Abstract

Interactive physical applications — virtual slicing, cross-sectional inspection, fragment dynamics — require an explicit interior, not a hollow shell. Existing approaches distil 2D diffusion priors, which low-pass filters structural membranes and demands per-object fine-tuning, or extend Gaussian splatting into the volume, where a plane has no exact intersection and a cut face carries boundary bleeding and unpainted voids.

We present a generative-free, pose-free framework that reconstructs cuttable 3D interiors from sparse unaligned cross-section photographs of different specimens. The volume is decoupled into a dual-grid boundary surface and a two-level latent occupancy lattice carrying one 8-dimensional feature per cell, with no rendering primitive inside. Cross-specimen inconsistency is reconciled by chordal alignment and moment-based silhouette fitting, without pose estimation. An arbitrary plane is cut analytically by intersecting it with lattice cell edges, giving a 2-manifold face rendered with the boundary in a single rasterisation pass.

The cut satisfies its plane equation to \(8.88\times10^{-16}\), leaves no unpainted pixel at 2048 px, and completes in 8.6–16.5 ms. Through one codec the representation is 10.0 MiB against 25.6 and 52.0 for two Gaussian baselines on the same object, and exposes a geometric and topological interface for background-grid solvers — integer adjacency, signed contact boundaries, exact component partitions and mass conservation by construction — without intermediate tetrahedralisation. Dynamic simulation is not attempted here.

1 Introduction

Standard 3D surface reconstruction methods fundamentally yield hollow shells. While visually compelling for standard novel view synthesis, they fail to support downstream physical and interactive applications — such as virtual planar slicing, non-destructive cross-sectional inspection, and the geometric stage of fracture simulation — which require an explicit, physically coherent, and high-fidelity internal volume. When an object is sliced, the revealed cross-section must exhibit crisp internal anatomy — membranes, fibres, pulp — rather than blurred approximations or empty voids.

Reconstructing realistic 3D interiors without volumetric CT scans remains an open challenge. Existing formulations predominantly face two core limitations.

  • Generative score distillation. Methods distilling 2D diffusion priors inevitably average score gradients over diffusion timesteps. This imposes an inherent low-pass filter on internal structures, washing out fine membrane details. While fine-tuning a diffusion model per object can recover structural fidelity, it necessitates an expensive, per-instance training pipeline.
  • Volumetric continuous primitives. Approaches extending 3D Gaussian Splatting to interiors struggle with the lack of explicit geometric boundaries. A planar cut through continuous Gaussians degrades into a heuristic primitive-culling step over a thick slab, causing severe boundary bleeding and leaving significant unpainted void artefacts — up to 8.29% at 2K resolution — where primitive scale drops below screen pixels.

To overcome these dilemmas, we present a generative-free, pose-free framework that reconstructs realistic 3D interiors directly from a handful of unaligned 2D cross-section photographs of different specimens, coupled with an exact, machine-precision planar cutting operator.

Our key insight is to decouple the surface boundary from the internal volume representation. For the exterior, we leverage a flexible dual grid that encodes sub-voxel surface contours. For the interior, we introduce a discrete two-level latent occupancy lattice carrying one compact latent feature per cell, eliminating internal rendering primitives entirely. We formulate interior supervision from unregistered cross-sections across disparate specimens by combining in-plane phase alignment with moment-based silhouette projection. Because all cutting planes query the shared latent lattice through a universal decoder, cross-specimen structural inconsistencies are naturally reconciled in 3D without camera pose estimation or generative hallucination. Furthermore, arbitrary planar cuts are resolved analytically by intersecting the cutting plane directly with lattice cell edges, guaranteeing strict 2-manifold cut faces rendered in a single differentiable rasterisation pass.

1.1 Primary contributions

  • Decoupled volumetric latent lattice. A decoupled geometry representation combining a dual-grid boundary surface with an interior two-level latent occupancy lattice, achieving compact storage — 10.0 MiB, a 61–81% compression over Gaussian baselines on the same object — without primitive-induced artefacts.
  • Pose-free cross-specimen supervision. A generative-free supervision pipeline that optimises 3D interior textures directly from unaligned, unposed 2D photographs of different specimens, via moment-based matching and a multi-plane expectation.
  • Closed-form machine-precision cutting. An analytical plane–lattice intersection operator that computes exact 2-manifold cut faces with machine-precision planarity, \(8.88\times10^{-16}\) residual, and 0% unpainted void pixels at 2048 px.
  • A solver interface, stated as geometry. The integer lattice supplies what a background-grid solver consumes — integer adjacency, signed collision boundaries, topologically exact component partitions, and mass conservation that follows from each cell belonging to exactly one component (Figure 4) — without intermediate tetrahedralisation. The claim is the interface, not a demonstrated simulation; no constitutive parameter is inferred and no dynamics are run (section 5.2).

The scope of the operator is the planar case: a half-space cut resolved in closed form. Curved and partial incisions are discussed in section 5.2.

2 Related work

2.1 3D interior generation and score distillation

Synthesising internal 3D structures from 2D priors has gained substantial traction with score distillation sampling (SDS) [27] and depth-conditioned diffusion models [26, 28]. For interior recovery, FruitNinja [3] adapts diffusion priors to supervise cut cross-sections and instantiates the volume with atomic 3D Gaussians [1]. SDS averages gradients across diffusion timesteps, imposing a low-pass filter that attenuates high-frequency biological membranes and fine fibres [3, 27]. Recovering such detail necessitates per-object diffusion fine-tuning [3], which is computationally expensive and admits hallucination. Solid texture synthesis [29] models volumes via stationary neighbourhood statistics, which by construction ignores axial convergence — citrus segments converging toward the poles.

Our method operates in a generative-free regime: where unposed 2D cross-section photographs of category exemplars are available, the latent occupancy is optimised directly, without intermediate diffusion models or per-object checkpoints, preserving membrane boundaries and avoiding generative hallucination.

2.2 Volumetric primitives against discrete latent lattices

Volumetric representations have evolved from dense voxel graphics and sparse grids [56–58, 60, 61] to continuous radiance fields [2, 16, 17] and structured 3D Gaussian Splatting [12–14]. InFusion [30] and InnerGS [31] leverage Gaussians for internal scene reconstruction. Continuous primitives possess infinite support and ill-defined boundaries: planar cutting through 3D Gaussians degrades into heuristic thresholding over a thick slab — in the reference implementation, an integral 5.63 coarse cells deep — producing boundary bleeding and unpainted void artefacts, up to 8.29% at 2K resolution, when primitive footprints fall below pixel scale. Neural implicit fields [2] require dense forward queries and provide no closed-form planar boundary.

We decouple the volume into a discrete two-level occupancy lattice carrying a single 8-dimensional latent per cell. Replacing internal splatting primitives with trilinear or tricubic lattice interpolation gives exact planar cuts, eliminates void artefacts, and reduces compressed storage to 10.0 MiB.

2.3 Surface extraction and dual grid encodings

Isosurface extraction traditionally relies on Marching Cubes [4] or feature-sensitive dual contouring [5–7], where dual vertices minimise quadric error metrics [8] to preserve sharp geometric features without staircase artefacts [64]. TRELLIS.2 [10, 11] introduced the field-free Flexible Dual Grid [63], placing dual vertices inside active voxels to compactly encode complex, non-manifold boundaries.

We adopt the official encoder [10] strictly for the outer boundary hull, keeping the internal lattice decoupled. During cutting, the exposed cross-section polygons and the dual-grid boundary are submitted to a unified differentiable rasteriser (nvdiffrast), resolving depth composition in a single pass without primitive conversion.

2.4 Virtual cutting and physical simulation

Interactive cutting has been explored in surgical simulation and fracture mechanics [48–51]. Classical approaches — the virtual node algorithm [48], polyhedral tetrahedral splitting [49], and extended finite elements [50] — rely on pre-existing volumetric meshes or scans, which are absent in photogrammetric radiance-field assets. PhysGaussian [18] and VR-GS [22] couple Gaussians with the Material Point Method [19–21], while GaussianFluent [23] densifies internal Gaussians to simulate mixed-material fracture with continuum damage [52].

Our two-level lattice provides integer cell connectivity, mass conservation and sign-based contact predicates directly, and closed-form polygon clipping evaluated in parallel gives a cut in 8.6–16.5 ms. What this supplies is the geometric and topological interface those solvers consume. Figure 4 drives one with it — the object is cut twice while falling and each discovered piece becomes its own MPM solver — but the dynamics are demonstrated, not validated: we report no measurement against a real fall, and the material parameters are chosen, not fitted.

3 Method

We decouple the volume into an explicit dual-grid surface boundary and an internal two-level latent occupancy lattice, removing continuous rendering primitives from the interior entirely. The representation is optimised directly from sparse, unposed 2D cross-section photographs and supports machine-precision planar cutting.

3.1 Decoupled volume representation

Let \(\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3\) be a bounded solid with boundary \(\partial\Omega\). We construct an axis-aligned two-level hierarchy \(\mathcal{V} = \mathcal{V}_0 \cup \mathcal{V}_1\): the coarse level \(\mathcal{V}_0\) spans the bounding box at spacing \(h_c = L/125\), and the fine level \(\mathcal{V}_1\) refines only the boundary band \(\{\mathbf{x} : \operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega) < \beta h_f\}\) with \(h_f = h_c/2\).

Figure 2 shows the four states these arrays take. The dual-grid exterior carries a dual vertex \(\mathbf{u}_v \in [0,1]^3\) and a split weight \(w_v \in [0,1]\) in each active surface voxel, capturing sub-voxel contours. Each interior cell stores only an 8-dimensional latent \(\mathbf{f}_i \in \mathbb{R}^8\); no rendering primitive is placed inside the volume. A value at an arbitrary \(\mathbf{x}\) is retrieved by interpolation \(\mathcal{I}\) over the neighbouring lattice nodes and decoded by the shared MLP:

$$ \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; g_\theta\!\Big( \mathcal{I}\big[\{\mathbf{f}_i\}\big](\mathbf{x}) \Big), \qquad \mathcal{I} \in \{\text{trilinear},\ \text{tricubic B-spline}\}. $$
(1)

Trilinear weights are \(C^0\): the value is continuous across a cell face and its derivative is not. The cubic B-spline basis over the same neighbourhood is \(C^2\) at eight times the gathers. Section 4.5 ablates the two.

3.2 Pose-free cross-specimen supervision

Given an unposed collection \(\mathcal{I}=\{\mathbf{I}_k\}_{k=1}^{M}\) of transverse (\(\mathcal{J}_\perp\)) and longitudinal (\(\mathcal{J}_\parallel\)) cross-sections of different specimens, we optimise \(\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{f}_i\}\) and \(\theta\) without recovering camera poses.

Global phase and chordal alignment. Transverse sections exhibit approximate radial coherence. In-plane rotation is resolved by circular cross-correlation of 1D angular luminance profiles \(a_k(\varphi)\),

$$ \delta_k \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{P}\arg\max_{m}\; \mathrm{DFT}^{-1}\big[\hat{a}_0\,\overline{\hat{a}_k}\big](m), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{I}}_k = \mathrm{Rot}_{-\delta_k}\mathbf{I}_k. $$
(2)

Transverse and longitudinal planes intersect in 1D chords \(\Pi_j \cap \Pi_{j'}\), each imaged twice. Inter-family alignment and the discrete assignment \(\pi \in S_M\) are resolved together by minimising chordal discrepancy:

$$ \min_{\pi,\;\{\delta_k\}} \; \sum_{j,\,j'} \big\lVert \mathbf{T}^{\delta}_{\pi(j)} - \mathbf{T}^{\delta}_{\pi(j')} \big\rVert_{\Pi_j \cap \Pi_{j'}}. $$
(3)

The problem is mixed: \(\pi\) is discrete, \(\{\delta_k\}\) continuous, and the chords couple them. It is solved exactly rather than relaxed — enumeration over \(S_M\) with coordinate descent over 72 candidate angles inside each — and offline. \(M \le 10\) here.

Moment-based target construction. During training a plane \(\Pi_j\) renders a section with silhouette \(\alpha_j\). A scale and translation are obtained from second-order spatial moments, warping the exemplar to the current geometry:

$$ s_j = \frac{r(\alpha_j)}{r(\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{k(j)})}, \qquad \mathbf{t}_j = c(\alpha_j) - s_j\,c(\tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{k(j)}), \qquad \mathbf{T}_j(\mathbf{u}) = \tilde{\mathbf{I}}_{k(j)}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{t}_j}{s_j}\right). $$
(4)

Remark (differentiability of the geometry fit). Equation (4) is a function of the rendered silhouette, so \(\mathbf{T}_j\) depends on \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\theta\). Differentiating through it admits a degenerate minimiser: the loss is reduced by shrinking \(\alpha_j\) rather than by matching content, since \(s_j\) rescales the target to follow. The target is therefore isolated by a stop-gradient, \(\mathrm{sg}[\mathbf{T}_j]\), which is what makes the moment fit a calibration rather than a free parameter of the optimisation.

Jittered expectation. A family of planes at fixed depths supervises a measure-zero subset of the volume. Replacing the nominal depth by a random variable \(d_j \sim \mathcal{U}(c_i - J\Delta,\, c_i + J\Delta)\), \(J = 0.5\), makes the objective an expectation over a continuous distribution of planes, estimated by one draw per plane per step. The supports then union to a slab of thickness \(\Delta\) about each nominal depth: measured, the fraction of solid cells receiving gradient rises from 32.2% to 95.1%, with 333 cells (0.0%) never reached.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_\perp \cup \mathcal{J}_\parallel} w_{q(j)}\,\mathbb{E}_{d_j}\!\Big[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sec}}\big( \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F},\theta;\Pi_j),\; \mathrm{sg}[\mathbf{T}_j]\big)\Big] \;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}} \;+\; \lambda_{\mathcal{S}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{skin}}. $$
(5)
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sec}}(R,T) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}} \Big[\,0.7\big(1-\mathrm{SSIM}(R_P,T_P)\big) + 0.3\lVert R_P-T_P \rVert_2^2 + 0.3\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{band}}(R_P,T_P)\,\Big]. $$
(6)

\(\mathcal{P}\) is six crops of 128 px inside the cut face's own silhouette, and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{band}}\) compares the distributions of four Gaussian-difference bands at \(\sigma \in \{0.5,1,2,4\}\) rather than their pixels: the target is a photograph of a different specimen, so its texture transfers and its pixel positions do not. The anisotropic prior penalises colour steps between face-sharing cells, weighted 4 on the two lattice axes perpendicular to the polar axis and 1 along it, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}}=0.1\); the skin term anchors the latents of boundary cells \(\mathcal{S}\) to the canonical exterior colours, \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{skin}} = |\mathcal{S}|^{-1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}} \lVert g_\theta(\mathbf{f}_v) - \hat{\mathbf{c}}_v \rVert_2^2\). Family weights \(w_q\) are inverse in each family's plane count. No perceptual network enters the objective.

Why cross-specimen supervision does not average to a blur. Equation (3) determines the assignment from the object's own geometry, not from a pose: a wrong \(\pi\) or \(\delta_k\) is visible as disagreement on chords that are imaged twice. Equation (1) then averages positionally rather than structurally — a cell holds one latent, so what two specimens agree on is written to the same cells and survives, while a seed particular to one specimen is written to cells the other does not touch and does not accumulate. The residual is the ghosting of section 5.2.

3.3 Closed-form planar cutting operator

Crossed-cell selection. Cells crossed by \(\Pi\) are identified in parallel by one comparison per cell, the separating-axis test for a plane against an axis-aligned box:

$$ \mathcal{B} \;=\; \Big\{\, i \;:\; \big|\mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{c}_i + d\big| \;\le\; \tfrac{1}{2}h\textstyle\sum_{a}|n_a| \,\Big\}, \qquad K = |\mathcal{B}|. $$
(7)

Uncrossed cells take the side sign \(q(i)=\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{c}_i+d)\), and connected components are maintained by union-find over sign-preserving face adjacency.

Analytic polygon formation. Within a crossed cell, each of the twelve edges \(e=(\mathbf{a}_e,\mathbf{b}_e)\) straddling the plane is solved for its exact root:

$$ \mathbf{v}_e = \mathbf{a}_e + t_e(\mathbf{b}_e-\mathbf{a}_e), \qquad t_e = \frac{-(\mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{a}_e + d)}{\mathbf{n}^\top(\mathbf{b}_e-\mathbf{a}_e)} \in [0,1]. $$
(8)

Figure 1 shows the operator's four states on real data. Sorting \(\{\mathbf{v}_e\}\) angularly about their centroid gives a convex polygon \(\mathcal{Q}_i\). Face-adjacent cells share their edge roots exactly, so \(\mathcal{Q} = \bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{Q}_i\) is closed. It is submitted with the dual-grid boundary to nvdiffrast, and depth composition is resolved in a single rasterisation pass. Vertex colours are differentiable in \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\theta\); vertex positions are determined by \(\Pi\) and the occupancy and take no gradient.

Algorithm 1 Closed-form planar cut and rasterisation
input  lattice \(\{C_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\), plane \(\Pi=(\mathbf{n},d)\)
output  polygons \(\{\mathcal{Q}_i\}\), triangles \(T\), labels \(\ell\)

1  \(s_i \leftarrow \mathbf{n}^\top\mathbf{c}_i + d\) for all \(i\), in parallel
2  \(\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \{\, i : |s_i| \le \tfrac{1}{2}h\sum_a|n_a| \,\}\) // separating-axis test, isolates the crossed band
3  for \(i \in \mathcal{B}\) in parallel
4   evaluate \(\sigma_a \sigma_b < 0\) over the twelve edges of \(C_i\)
5   \(\mathbf{v}_e \leftarrow \mathbf{p}_a + \tfrac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_a-\sigma_b}(\mathbf{p}_b-\mathbf{p}_a)\) // exact roots, equation (8)
6   \(\mathcal{Q}_i \leftarrow \mathrm{sort\_by\_angle}(\{\mathbf{v}_e\})\)
7   \(T \leftarrow T \cup \mathrm{fan}(\mathcal{Q}_i)\), vertex colours from equation (1)
8  \(\ell \leftarrow \mathrm{union\_find}\big(\mathrm{sign}(s_i),\ \text{face adjacency}\big)\)
9  return \(\{\mathcal{Q}_i\}, T, \ell\)

3.4 Theoretical guarantees

Proposition 1 (manifold boundary). Under equal-size subdivision with \(h_f = h_c/2\) and the level of a cell fixed in advance, two face-adjacent cells differ by at most one level and their shared face is tiled by either one face or four coincident quarter-faces. No edge of a finer face terminates in the interior of a coarser one, so the cut boundary is closed and the extracted surface is 2-manifold.

Proof. The two-level scheme assigns level 1 to boundary-band cells and level 0 elsewhere, so the level difference across a face lies in \(\{0,1\}\). At difference 0 the faces coincide. At difference 1 the four fine faces partition the coarse face exactly, since \(h_f\) divides it into \(2\times2\) congruent squares on the same integer lattice. A T-junction requires a fine vertex interior to a coarse edge and not shared with the coarse cell, which the alignment excludes. \(\square\)

Proposition 2 (cost). Extracting the cut geometry and the connected components of the two sides is \(\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{B}|) = \mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{crossed}})\), not \(\mathcal{O}(N)\).

Proof. Line 2 selects \(\mathcal{B}\) by one \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) comparison per cell, evaluated on the whole array at once. Within each selected cell the twelve edges give at most twelve roots, sorted into one convex polygon in \(\mathcal{O}(1)\). Side membership is the sign of the same expression, so union-find merges are confined to the crossed band and cells away from it retain the labelling of the uncut object. \(\square\)

The bound describes the geometry stage, which is where the work is: selection, root-finding and polygon assembly touch \(|\mathcal{B}|\) cells and nothing else. The measured wall-clock time carries an additive term in \(N\) that is not algorithmic — adjacency and component arrays are allocated across the whole leaf set once per cut rather than over the band — so the constant, not the order, is what a persistent adjacency buffer would remove (section 4.2).

Holding that buffer persistent across cuts, which nothing in the formulation prevents, leaves only the \(\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{B}|)\) geometry stage of Proposition 2.

Anisotropic smoothness does nothing. Its largest effect on any object is 0.0065 and its mean effect is \(-0.0010\), at the seed repeat. It is retained in the released configuration only because it is free; nothing in Table 5 argues for it.

Reference sampling is the one term that generalises. It improves five of the seven objects, by 0.0404 on the cake and 0.0402 on the pomegranate, and the two it does not improve it leaves alone — \(+0.0078\) on the apple and \(+0.0004\) on the doughnut. The critic is the arm the orange would have sold us. On the orange it is the best configuration in the table, \(-0.0051\) beyond reference sampling, and on the loaf and the pomegranate it improves again. Across all seven it is a regression: \(+0.0053\) on the mean, with \(+0.0301\) on the cake, \(+0.0167\) on the melon and \(+0.0156\) on the doughnut. One object cannot decide a term that is applied to every object, and it is off in the released configuration for that reason and no other.

Interpolation continuity. Raising the lattice interpolation from trilinear \(C^0\) to cubic B-spline \(C^2\) sharpens the supervised longitudinal face from 0.0919 to 0.0858, the best of any configuration, at 0.0122 on the held-out longitudinal column. A continuous derivative across cell faces suppresses the crease that a \(C^0\) basis leaves at every boundary.

Discrete lattice against a monolithic field. Replacing the per-cell latent with a single decoder evaluated at the sample point degrades the supervised longitudinal column from 0.0919 to 0.1585, a 72% loss. One smooth function serving every sample point cannot fit the planes that do have photographs; the lattice's positional decoupling is what routes gradient to local structure.

Differentiability of the geometry fit. Allowing gradient into the similarity transform of equation (4) admits the degenerate minimiser predicted in section 3.2: the target is driven to white background and only the skin survives, at 0.5585 against 0.0763 in the formulation where it was measured. Fixing the transform analytically from moments is what makes the supervision well-posed.

Six of eleven interventions do nothing measurable. The field prior's direction and weight, a second-order prior, the low-discrepancy schedule and a single photograph for every azimuth all sit inside the seed repeat on every column.

4.4 Structural anisotropy between the two families

Table 2 shows transverse planes generalising to unobserved depths at no cost and longitudinal planes carrying a gap of \(+0.038\) to \(+0.096\). The two families do not sample the volume alike. The transverse family stacks along the polar axis and its jittered planes sweep a band; the longitudinal family fans about the axis, so its coverage is radial spokes and a cell between two spokes receives gradient only when a jittered plane reaches across. Over the orange 94.4% of cells are reached, the median cell 9 times and the busiest 492.

The gap is not a spectral aliasing artefact. If the angular sampling behaved as a comb of \(P\) teeth, transverse renders would carry a peak at \(m=P\); measured against a local baseline the prominence there is 1.00 on the orange and 1.12 on the watermelon, and the most prominent frequency is \(m=33\) and \(m=11\) (Figure 5).

The gap is also confounded with offset. The available longitudinal references are central sections, so the supervised planes of that family never leave the neighbourhood of the axis — 1.84–2.92 coarse cells from the centre on the orange — while the held-out planes reach 2.22–7.73 and span 55.6° of azimuth against 169.4°. The reported gap therefore measures extrapolation away from the axis at least as much as interpolation between azimuths. An offset-matched split would separate the two. The full spectral analysis and the eleven interventions are in the technical appendices.

4.5 Per-object configuration

Two quantities are properties of an object's acquisition rather than free parameters, and both are determined from the object itself; Figure 6 shows what each object is set up as, and Table 6 the measurement behind the first.

The polar axis is measured, not labelled: the occupancy is rotated about a candidate direction and the fraction landing back inside itself is counted, and the direction maximising it is taken. On the four objects whose stored axis is independently known to be correct the procedure recovers it to within 6°, which is what makes its answer usable on the rest. The axis then fixes the plane split, which follows the object's extent — a short axis takes fewer transverse planes and more longitudinal ones.

5 Conclusion and limitations

5.1 Conclusion

We have presented a generative-free framework that reconstructs cuttable 3D objects directly from sparse, unposed 2D cross-section photographs of different specimens. Decoupling the visible boundary into a flexible dual grid and the internal volume into a discrete two-level latent occupancy lattice removes rendering primitives from the interior entirely.

Pose-free supervision — chordal alignment for the discrete assignment, moment-based fitting for the in-plane transform, and a multi-plane expectation for coverage — reconciles cross-specimen variation in 3D without per-instance diffusion fine-tuning or score distillation. The closed-form cutting operator evaluates exact cell-edge intersections, giving machine-precision planarity, \(8.88\times10^{-16}\) residual, and strict 2-manifold continuity with no unpainted pixel at 2048 px. At 10.0 MiB and 8.6–16.5 ms per cut, the lattice provides an exact, mass-conserving topological interface for background-grid solvers.

5.2 Limitations and future work

  • Ghosting under non-rigid variation between specimens. Supervision absorbs global scale, translation and in-plane rotation through a 2D similarity transform. Anatomical deformation beyond that — compressed crumb, cake layers not parallel to their own base — leaves membranes unaligned, and cells shared by two references hold their mean, which appears as a doubled or smeared boundary rather than a misplaced one. It bounds the loaf and the cake in Table 2, 0.4065 and 0.4664 shown-transverse against the orange's 0.1238, and more supervision does not remove it: the disagreement is in the data. A chord-conditioned deformation field applied before lattice pooling is the natural extension.
  • The half-space formulation. Components and contact boundaries follow from a global sign over a plane. An incision terminating inside the volume, or a curved cut, would require a cell to belong to more than one piece, which the integer labelling cannot express without over-partitioning. Virtual-node duplication or per-element state tracking is the standard route.
  • Geometric readiness, not constitutive identification. The lattice supplies integer adjacency, signed collision boundaries and mass conservation, and its contact normals deviate from radial by 36.0° at the median against 49.0° for a cube collider at the same cell size. Appearance and material clustering are optimised; no Young's modulus or fracture toughness is inferred, and no dynamic simulation is run here. Coupling the lattice with differentiable parameter estimation is open.

A further constraint is evaluative rather than representational: the available longitudinal references are central sections, so held-out planes of that family are drawn from a band the supervised planes never occupy (section 4.4), and an offset-matched split is needed before the longitudinal gap can be attributed.
